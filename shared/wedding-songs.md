# Wedding Songs — The Occasion Is the Setting, Never the Subject

Pull this file before writing ANY wedding or couple song. Established 2026-08-16 from the "Long Way To You" result (Barber wedding film, first accepted vocal track after two rejections).

---

## The Revelation, Stated Precisely

Kyle's read on why "Long Way To You" worked: "i think its because its about weddings."

Verified reality: the accepted lyric contains **zero wedding vocabulary**. All 157 words were machine-checked. No wedding, bride, groom, vow, ring, altar, aisle, marriage, husband, wife, veil, church, forever, or "I do." That was a deliberate constraint Kyle himself set ("do not make the song about a wedding though").

So the true law is the inversion, and it is the same law that made "Every Weekend's the Fourth of July" work:

> **The occasion is the destination of the story, not the subject of the lyrics.**
> The song narrates the human arc that ends at the occasion. The footage supplies the occasion. When the lyric names the occasion, the film says everything twice and both copies get weaker.

"Long Way To You" is *functionally* about a wedding: a restless man stops running because one person became home. Cut over getting-ready footage, the wedding context does the naming. That is why it feels "about weddings" while containing none of the words.

Two data points now confirm the law (July 4th song, wedding song). Treat it as standing doctrine for occasion songs: holidays, weddings, anniversaries, graduations, retirements.

## Why It Worked — Full Autopsy

### 1. The narrator arc is the couple's real shape
Restless guy, one-stoplight town, could not name what he wanted, stops running. Verse 2 carries the turn per `shared/songwriting-craft.md`: "love was a fire you chase" inverts into "you're a porch light left on down the road." One image does the whole job. The arc was grounded in primary sources (the couple's audio-guestbook voicemails, the contract, the venue), not invented. Never invent family stakes (see the dead-dad incident in `prompts/2026-07-04-every-weekends-the-fourth-of-july.md`).

### 2. Wordless hook = editorial space
The hook is "oh-oh." This is a film-scoring decision disguised as a songwriting decision. Wordless singalong sections carry no semantic load, so real audio (a father's first-look reaction, voicemail greetings) sits ON TOP of the chorus without fighting lyrics. The editor drops dialogue over the oh-oh sections and lets lyric lines land where the footage breathes. Any song destined for a film should budget wordless sections on purpose.

### 3. One key family, tempo ladder
Everything in the Barber film lives in D: prelude 71 BPM, meeting instrumental 96, this song 116. Cross-fades stay clean and the film climbs in energy instead of jumping. When scoring a multi-song film, declare the key family and tempo ladder FIRST, then write each piece into its rung.

### 4. Vocal direction by performance, never throat texture
The first version of this exact song was rejected as "too ai... too raspy." Same lyrics. The only change was the vocal direction. What fixed it:

- Era tag up front: "1970s live-to-tape folk country session." Single highest-leverage phrase.
- "micro-timing variation, less quantized, players pushing and pulling the beat together."
- Performance cues: conversational, talking to one person across the room, a hair behind the beat, audible breath pickups, micro-pauses after commas, no vibrato, soft verses / open chorus, single take, dry and forward.
- Achievable ensemble: "two friends join in rough unison on the chorus only," never "big untrained group vocals" (Suno fakes crowds by multitracking one processed voice).
- Style Influence MEDIUM, not Strong. Over-obedience reads synthetic.
- **Throat-texture words are banned: raspy, husky, gravel, gritty, crack, weathered.** Do not even negate them ("no rasp" summons rasp). This supersedes the older Character Prompt example in `shared/vocal-direction.md` and the "no raspy vocals, no gravel" negatives in the July 4 package for all Kyle work: never introduce the concept at all.

## Pre-Write Checklist (run every wedding song)

1. Pull the couple's real material first: voicemails, transcripts, contract, venue geography. Verify every concrete detail. Mark inferences.
2. Write the arc that ENDS at the occasion. Zero ceremony vocabulary unless the client explicitly asks for it. Machine-check the finished lyric against the banned list: wedding, bride, groom, vow, ring, altar, aisle, marry/marriage, husband, wife, veil, church, I do.
3. Give the hook a wordless option (oh-oh, hey, mm) sized for real-audio overlay.
4. Confirm the film's key family and tempo ladder; write into the open rung.
5. Vocal direction: era tag, less-quantized language, performance cues only, achievable ensemble, Style Influence Medium. No throat-texture words anywhere, positive or negative.
6. Two-box rule and 600-900 char style box per `models/suno/advanced-prompting.md`. Exclusions at the end, 3-5, never naming vocal texture.
